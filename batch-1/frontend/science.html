Can you trust what blinks?

A blink tool is only useful if a moving dot is really moving and a brightening star is really brightening. This page shows how ORBITAL avoids the traps in SPHEREx data, and checks its own measurements against independent catalogues.

In 90 seconds. Every Sky Probe image comes live from NASA/IPAC IRSA (SPHEREx DR1 + QR3). We put older NASA images (2MASS ≈1999, AllWISE ≈2010) on the same pixel grid, so a star's position can be compared over ~27 years instead of one. Measured that way, the motions of the fast-moving challenge targets agree with the Gaia/literature values: all five fast movers (Barnard's Star, Epsilon Indi B, WISE 0855, Luhman 16, Proxima Centauri) come out within 1.2σ of the catalogue on a 16-year AllWISE baseline, and Barnard's Star at 10.6 ± 0.25″/yr against 10.4. We also refuse to call a brightness change "real" unless both frames saw the star at the same wavelength.

01The same-colour rule (linear variable filters)

SPHEREx does not take a picture through one filter. Each of its six detectors (D1–D6, 0.75–5.0 µm) sits under a linear variable filter: a filter whose colour changes smoothly from one edge to the other. So a star's wavelength depends on where on the detector it lands, and that changes from visit to visit (SPHEREx Explanatory Supplement).

As a result, two frames can capture the same star at, say, 1.60 µm and 1.75 µm. If that star is redder than its neighbours, it will appear to brighten even when nothing has changed. ORBITAL reads each frame's wavelength at the target from the file's own wavelength table, shows it on every frame, and:

  • the Same colour only switch plays only frames within 1 % in wavelength of the current one;
  • the difference image refuses pairs more than 1 % apart and offers the closest matching pair instead.

Why 1 %: the spectral resolution of D1–D4 is about R ≈ 40 (one resolution element ≈ 2.5 % of λ) , so 1 % keeps both frames inside one element. This is our choice, not a mission rule.

02Sub-pixel motion and the 27-year baseline

A SPHEREx pixel is 6.15″ (arc-seconds; 1″ = 1/3600 of a degree). Barnard's Star, the fastest star in the sky, moves 10.4″ per year, so between two SPHEREx visits six months apart it moves less than one pixel. Within SPHEREx alone, almost every star motion is sub-pixel: real, but too small to see by blinking.

The fix is time. ORBITAL fetches 2MASS (1997–2001) and AllWISE (2010–11) images of the same patch from IRSA, resamples them onto the SPHEREx grid, and lets you blink 1999 → 2026. Over 27 years Barnard's Star moves ≈ 280″ ≈ 45 pixels. The Measure tool then centroids the star (finds the brightness-weighted centre to a fraction of a pixel) in both images and converts the shift into a proper motion, with an uncertainty budget:

σ = √(2·σc² + (μ·σt)²) / Δt, with σc = 0.3 px per centroid, σt = ±0.3 yr for AllWISE (a stack of 2010–11 scans) and ±0.05 yr for 2MASS. Parallax (the yearly back-and-forth caused by Earth's orbit, up to 0.77″ here) is not removed; over 16–27 years it adds at most 30–50 mas/yr.

03Epoch propagation

A catalogue position is true only at its epoch (the date it refers to). For a moving star ORBITAL moves the position to the date of every frame: Δδ = μδ·Δt north and Δα = μα*·Δt / cos δ east (μα* already includes cos δ). That is why the Probe centres Barnard's Star at its 2026 position, ~4.5′ from its J2000 one, and why the default search radius grows with the motion (radius = μ × 28 yr + 0.02°: 0.10° for Barnard's Star, 0.08° for WISE 0855) so that both the 1999 and 2026 positions fit.

04Flags: what the camera says not to trust

Every SPHEREx file carries a FLAGS layer (one bit per problem per pixel). ORBITAL reads the bit names from each file's own header — never hard-coded — and masks transients, overflow, non-linear, hot, cold, persistence, ghosts and similar pixels. Bright stars that saturate are therefore shown as holes, honestly, instead of as fake flat tops. The Flags view lists what was masked per frame.

05Paired detectors

SPHEREx splits the light with a dichroic (a mirror that reflects short wavelengths and passes long ones) so that two detectors look at the same sky at the same moment: D1+D4, D2+D5 and D3+D6. A real moving object is at the same place in both members of a pair at the same time; a cosmic-ray hit appears in only one. Brightness changes between bands of one pair are colour, not time.

06Known asteroids

"Known asteroids" asks NASA/JPL's Small-Body Identification service which catalogued asteroids and comets were in the patch on each SPHEREx date, and moves each one to the exact exposure time with its rates. Units checked live: rates are in arc-seconds per hour, and the RA rate is already on-sky (it includes cos Dec): for 8 asteroids at Dec ≈ +35°, two answers 2 hours apart moved by the rate × 2 h only after the cos Dec scaling (agreement 2.6″ vs 9.8″ without it). Objects whose position error is over 10′ are not drawn.

07Validation: do we measure what Gaia measures?

We ran the Measure method on all 19 targets with live IRSA data and compared with the catalogue proper motion (Gaia DR3 or the literature; WISE 0855 from Luhman & Esplin 2016). Stars that should not move act as a null test: they must come out at ≈ 0. Extended or saturated objects are marked n/a — a centroid means nothing for a nebula.

08Known limits (what we do not claim)

  • Proper motions are a check, not new science. Gaia measures them ~100× better. Our point: SPHEREx data plus older NASA surveys reproduce them, in a browser, for anyone.
  • Centroids are measured on display images (8-bit, stretched) in the browser. Saturated cores (Mira, Betelgeuse, R Dra) bias them; the astropy check above uses the raw data.
  • Parallax is not modelled (≤ 0.77″, a ≤ 50 mas/yr effect over our baselines).
  • Brightness across missions is not comparable. 2MASS, WISE and SPHEREx have different filters and scales; we compare positions across missions, brightness only within SPHEREx at matched wavelength.
  • Deep fields: the Probe searches the DR1 and QR3 collections (at the north ecliptic pole: 439 + 232 images per band set). IRSA also publishes a separate spherex_dr1_deep collection (≥ 20,000 entries there) that we do not yet search.
  • Star Vault uses a fixed copy of IRSA files cut ~3′ around each target, so long-baseline blinks of fast movers need the Sky Probe.
  • Asteroid predictions are JPL's fast two-body positions; error bars are shown when over 1 pixel.
